Abstract

Listeria monocytogenes poses a persistent threat to global public health, largely due to its exceptional ability to thrive at refrigeration temperatures [1]—a trait that renders many conventional detection methods inadequate for rapid, on‑site screening. To address this, we engineered a dual‑input "AND‑gate" biosensing platform that selectively detects viable L. monocytogenes. The first input is the pore‑forming toxin Listeriolysin O (LLO), secreted exclusively by metabolically active bacteria, which disrupts cholesterol‑optimized synthetic liposomes or surface‑engineered HEK293T‑derived exosomes [2]. The second input is a blue‑light‑triggered LOV domain that drives proximity‑dependent complementation of split‑sfGFP [3], or enables TEV protease‑mediated cleavage of a quenched EGFP‑REACh reporter for robust signal amplification [4]. Our integrated mathematical modeling calibrates multi‑channel fluorescence kinetics to enable precise bacterial quantification. Throughout the design process, we engaged with food safety authorities and industry stakeholders to ensure biological containment, user‑friendliness, and portability. The resulting modular biosensor distinguishes viable from dead cells without requiring expensive instrumentation or specialized training, and delivers results within a single day—offering a practical, deployable toolkit for enhancing real‑time food safety surveillance.

Inspiration

Inspiration often comes from the most ordinary moments. Our team member Skye's younger brother fell ill after eating raw salmon fillets bought from their local supermarket—a product they had purchased many times before. He developed diarrhea and a fever. They were puzzled, but after some medication his symptoms subsided, and the matter was soon forgotten. Two days later, a news report caught their attention: A local food safety agency urgently recalled smoked salmon contaminated with Listeria. That made Skye pause. Looking into the pathogen, our team learned that L. monocytogenes is capable of active growth at refrigeration temperatures—meaning even cold-stored fresh produce is not free from risk. Yet conventional detection methods are largely confined to laboratories, requiring days to yield results. This realization prompted a question: could we build a simple, rapid biosensor that detects viable Listeria on site, accessible to anyone without specialized training?

Background

Listeria monocytogenes is a Gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic bacterium widely distributed in nature. Its defining feature is its remarkable cold tolerance: the pathogen grows across a temperature range of 2–42°C, with active multiplication even at standard refrigeration temperatures [5]. At 0°C it can still grow slowly, and at −20°C it can survive for up to a year [6]. This "refrigerator predator" makes cold storage not only fails to eliminate the risk but can actually allow the bacteria to proliferate to dangerous levels over time.

The global demand for fresh and ready-to-eat foods has made this threat increasingly widespread. In just the past two years, there have been multiple news reports around the world about food contaminated with Listeria: Italy urgently recalled smoked salmon from the market [8]; the U.S. pulled nearly 368,000 pounds of turkey bacon [9]; and in Macau, routine inspections detected unacceptable levels of the pathogen in ready-to-eat octopus salad, leading to immediate sales suspensions [10].

While healthy individuals typically experience only mild, self-limiting symptoms, L. monocytogenes poses devastating risks to high-risk populations. In pregnant women, the bacterium can cross the placental barrier and infect the fetus, leading to miscarriage, stillbirth, premature delivery, or severe neonatal infections [11]. For the elderly and immunocompromised, infection can progress to bacteremia, meningitis, and death [12]. Despite these severe consequences, conventional detection methods leave a critical gap in food safety surveillance that our project seeks to address.

Current Solution

Despite severe consequences caused by L. monocytogenes, current detection methods leave a critical gap in food safety surveillance, which is rooted in four major drawbacks.

Time‑consuming

The gold‑standard culture‑based method (ISO 11290‑1) requires 5–7 days to yield a definitive result [13]. This prolonged turnaround time renders it impractical for short‑shelf‑life products, as food may have been consumed or expired long before contamination is confirmed.

Poor specificity

For culture-based methods, it is difficult to distinguish L. monocytogenes from other potentially harmless Listeria species that form similar colonies on selective plates [14]. Likewise, immunoassays suffer from false positives due to antibody cross-reactivity with non-pathogenic Listeria species such as L. innocua [15].

Inability to distinguish viable from dead cells

This is the key drawback of conventional methods. Molecular techniques like PCR amplify DNA indiscriminately from both live and dead cells, generating false‑positive signals that misrepresent actual health risks [17]. Conversely, culture methods cannot detect viable but non‑culturable (VBNC) cells, creating blind spots in routine surveillance [18]. Although some innovative approaches today can achieve this, they often do so at the expense of other aspects.

High entry barrier

Molecular assays usually require expensive instrumentation, precise temperature control, and well‑trained personnel, making them unsuitable for resource‑limited settings or field deployment [19]. This dependency on centralized laboratories severely restricts their accessibility for routine, large‑scale food screening.
