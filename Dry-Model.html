Predictive Modeling of L. monocytogenes

1. Background and Objective

Listeria monocytogenes is an important foodborne pathogen that can survive and grow under refrigerated conditions. This makes it a major concern in ready-to-eat (RTE) foods, especially meat, poultry, seafood, salads, and other processed food products. Since RTE foods are usually consumed without further heating, the growth of L. monocytogenes during storage may directly increase food safety risks.

The objective of this model is to establish a predictive microbiology framework for describing and predicting the growth behavior of L. monocytogenes under food-related environmental conditions. The model combines two levels of modeling:

  1. A primary growth model, which describes how bacterial concentration changes with time.
  2. A secondary environmental model, which describes how environmental factors affect the maximum specific growth rate.

In this framework, experimental or literature-based data such as storage temperature, pH, water activity, salt concentration, nitrite concentration, and organic acid concentration are used to predict the growth rate of L. monocytogenes. The predicted growth rate is then inserted into a primary growth model to simulate the bacterial growth curve over time.

2. Overall Modeling Framework

The complete modeling framework can be summarized as follows:

  1. Collect food matrix parameters, including temperature, pH, water activity, salt, nitrite, lactate, acetate, diacetate, and propionate.
  2. Convert each environmental factor into a gamma inhibition factor.
  3. Calculate the predicted maximum specific growth rate, $\mu_{\max}$, using the Gamma concept model.
  4. Use $\mu_{\max}$ and the lag time parameter in a primary model such as the Logistic-with-delay model or the Baranyi model.
  5. Generate the predicted growth curve of L. monocytogenes, usually expressed as $\log(\text{CFU/g})$ versus time.

3. Primary Growth Models

3.1 Purpose of Primary Models

Primary models describe the change in bacterial population over time under a fixed set of environmental conditions. In the case of L. monocytogenes, the bacterial concentration is usually expressed as $\log(\text{CFU/g})$ or $\ln(\text{CFU/g})$. The input data for the primary model are time and colony count, while the main outputs are:

  • $\mu_{\max}$: maximum specific growth rate
  • $\lambda$ or $t_{\text{lag}}$: lag time
  • $N_0$: initial bacterial concentration
  • $N_{\max}$: maximum population density

A typical bacterial growth curve contains three main phases:

  1. Lag phase: bacteria adapt to the food environment and show little increase in population.
  2. Exponential phase: bacteria grow rapidly at the maximum growth rate.
  3. Stationary phase: growth slows down and the population approaches the maximum density.

3.2 Logistic-with-Delay Model

The Logistic-with-delay model is used to describe bacterial growth with an explicit lag phase. Before the lag time, the bacterial population remains approximately constant. After the lag phase, the population increases according to a logistic growth pattern.

$$\log N(t) = \begin{cases} \log N_0, & t < t_{\text{lag}} \\[8pt] \log\left( \dfrac{N_{\max}}{1 + \left(\dfrac{N_{\max}}{N_0} - 1\right) \exp\left[-\mu_{\max} (t - t_{\text{lag}})\right]} \right), & t \geq t_{\text{lag}} \end{cases}$$

where:

  • $t$ is storage time;
  • $N(t)$ is the bacterial concentration at time $t$;
  • $N_0$ is the initial bacterial concentration;
  • $N_{\max}$ is the maximum population density;
  • $\mu_{\max}$ is the maximum specific growth rate;
  • $t_{\text{lag}}$ is the lag time.

This model is simple and biologically interpretable. It clearly separates the lag phase from the growth phase. It is useful when the experimental data show an obvious delay before bacterial growth begins.

3.3 Baranyi Model

The Baranyi model is another commonly used primary model in predictive microbiology. Compared with the Logistic-with-delay model, the Baranyi model provides a smoother transition from the lag phase to the exponential phase. This is important because L. monocytogenes cannot move instantly from physiological adaptation to rapid growth. Instead, there is a gradual transition period.

$$y(t) = y_0 + \mu_{\max} A(t) - \ln\left(1 + \frac{\exp(\mu_{\max} A(t)) - 1}{\exp(y_{\max} - y_0)}\right)$$

where the adjustment function $A(t)$ is given by:

$$A(t) = t + \frac{1}{\mu_{\max}} \ln\left[\exp(-\mu_{\max} t) + \exp(-h_0) - \exp(-\mu_{\max} t - h_0)\right]$$

In this model:

  • $y(t)$ is the bacterial concentration at time $t$, usually expressed as $\ln(\text{CFU/g})$;
  • $y_0$ is the initial bacterial concentration;
  • $y_{\max}$ is the maximum bacterial concentration;
  • $\mu_{\max}$ is the maximum specific growth rate;
  • $A(t)$ is the adjustment function;
  • $h_0$ describes the initial physiological state of the bacterial cells.

The function $A(t)$ accounts for the physiological adaptation of bacterial cells during the lag phase. Therefore, the Baranyi model is especially useful when the transition between lag phase and exponential phase needs to be represented more realistically.

4. Secondary Model: Gamma Concept Model

4.1 Purpose of the Secondary Model

While primary models describe growth over time, they do not directly explain how food properties and storage conditions affect bacterial growth. For this reason, a secondary model is used to describe the relationship between environmental parameters and the maximum specific growth rate $\mu_{\max}$.

In this study, the Gamma concept model is adopted. The basic idea of the Gamma model is that each environmental factor contributes an inhibition factor, denoted as $\gamma$. Each $\gamma$ value ranges from 0 to 1:

  • $\gamma = 1$ means the condition is optimal or nearly optimal for growth;
  • $0 < \gamma < 1$ means the condition partially inhibits growth;
  • $\gamma = 0$ means growth is completely inhibited.

The predicted maximum specific growth rate is calculated as:

$$\mu_{\max} = \mu_{\text{opt}} \cdot \gamma(T) \cdot \gamma(\text{pH}) \cdot \gamma(a_w) \cdot \gamma(\text{Nit}) \cdot \gamma(\text{HA}) \cdot \xi(T, \text{pH}, a_w, \text{Nit}, \text{HA})$$

where:

  • $\mu_{\text{opt}}$ is the maximum specific growth rate under optimal conditions;
  • $\gamma(T)$ describes the effect of temperature;
  • $\gamma(\text{pH})$ describes the effect of acidity;
  • $\gamma(a_w)$ describes the effect of water activity;
  • $\gamma(\text{Nit})$ describes the inhibitory effect of nitrite;
  • $\gamma(\text{HA})$ describes the inhibitory effect of undissociated organic acids;
  • $\xi$ is a multi-factor interaction term.

4.2 Temperature Effect

Temperature is one of the most important factors controlling the growth of L. monocytogenes. The temperature gamma factor can be written as:

$$\gamma(T) = \begin{cases} 0, & T \leq T_{\min} \\[6pt] \left(\dfrac{T - T_{\min}}{T_{\text{opt}} - T_{\min}}\right)^2, & T_{\min} < T < T_{\text{opt}} \\[6pt] 1, & T \geq T_{\text{opt}} \end{cases}$$

where:

  • $T_{\min}$ is the minimum temperature for growth;
  • $T_{\text{opt}}$ is the optimal temperature for growth;
  • $T$ is the storage temperature.

For refrigerated foods, such as those stored at 4°C, $\gamma(T)$ is usually much smaller than 1. This means that refrigeration slows down bacterial growth but may not completely stop the growth of L. monocytogenes.

4.3 pH Effect

The pH gamma factor describes how acidity or alkalinity affects bacterial growth:

$$\gamma(\text{pH}) = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{pH} \leq \text{pH}_{\min} \\[6pt] \dfrac{(\text{pH} - \text{pH}_{\min})(\text{pH}_{\max} - \text{pH})}{(\text{pH}_{\text{opt}} - \text{pH}_{\min})(\text{pH}_{\max} - \text{pH}_{\text{opt}})}, & \text{pH}_{\min} < \text{pH} < \text{pH}_{\max} \\[6pt] 0, & \text{pH} \geq \text{pH}_{\max} \end{cases}$$

where:

  • $\text{pH}_{\min}$ is the minimum pH for growth;
  • $\text{pH}_{\text{opt}}$ is the optimal pH for growth;
  • $\text{pH}_{\max}$ is the maximum pH for growth.

For many RTE meat products, the pH is approximately between 5.8 and 6.8. This range usually allows the growth of L. monocytogenes, although growth may be slower than under optimal pH conditions.

4.4 Water Activity Effect

Water activity, denoted as $a_w$, measures the amount of water available for microbial growth. The water activity gamma factor can be written as:

$$\gamma(a_w) = \begin{cases} 0, & a_w < a_{w,\min} \\[6pt] \dfrac{a_w - a_{w,\min}}{a_{w,\text{opt}} - a_{w,\min}}, & a_{w,\min} \leq a_w < a_{w,\text{opt}} \\[6pt] 1, & a_w \geq a_{w,\text{opt}} \end{cases}$$

where:

  • $a_{w,\min}$ is the minimum water activity required for growth;
  • $a_{w,\text{opt}}$ is the optimal water activity;
  • $a_w$ is the water activity of the food product.

Most RTE meat and seafood products have high water activity values, often around 0.95–0.99. Therefore, water activity alone may not strongly inhibit L. monocytogenes unless it is reduced by drying, salting, or other preservation methods.

4.5 Nitrite Effect

Nitrite is commonly used in processed meat products as a preservative. Its inhibitory effect can be expressed as:

$$\gamma(\text{Nit}) = \begin{cases} 1 - \dfrac{\text{Nit}}{\text{MIC}_{\text{Nit}}}, & \text{Nit} < \text{MIC}_{\text{Nit}} \\[8pt] 0, & \text{Nit} \geq \text{MIC}_{\text{Nit}} \end{cases}$$

where:

  • $\text{Nit}$ is the nitrite concentration;
  • $\text{MIC}_{\text{Nit}}$ is the minimum inhibitory concentration of nitrite.

As nitrite concentration increases, $\gamma(\text{Nit})$ decreases. This means that higher nitrite concentration produces stronger inhibition of bacterial growth.

4.6 Organic Acid Effect

Organic acids and their salts, such as lactate, acetate, diacetate, and propionate, are often used in RTE meat products to inhibit microbial growth. The total organic acid inhibition factor can be represented as:

$$\gamma(\text{HA}) = \gamma(\text{HA}_{\text{acetic}}) \cdot \gamma(\text{HA}_{\text{diacetate}}) \cdot \gamma(\text{HA}_{\text{lactic}}) \cdot \gamma(\text{HA}_{\text{propionic}})$$

For each undissociated organic acid, the inhibition factor can be written generally as:

$$\gamma(\text{HA}_i) = \begin{cases} 1 - \dfrac{[\text{HA}_i]}{\text{MIC}_i}, & [\text{HA}_i] < \text{MIC}_i \\[8pt] 0, & [\text{HA}_i] \geq \text{MIC}_i \end{cases}$$

where:

  • $[\text{HA}_i]$ is the concentration of the undissociated form of organic acid $i$;
  • $\text{MIC}_i$ is the minimum inhibitory concentration of that acid.

The use of multiple organic acids can produce a combined inhibitory effect. For example, lactate and diacetate are frequently used together in RTE meat products to suppress the growth of L. monocytogenes.

5. Use of Food Matrix Parameters

5.1 Role of the Parameter Table

The food matrix parameter table provides the environmental inputs required by the Gamma concept model. These data include: food product type, storage temperature, pH, water activity, salt concentration, acetate concentration, diacetate concentration, lactate concentration, propionate concentration, nitrite concentration, and packaging condition.

These parameters are not the final model output. Instead, they are used as model inputs. Each parameter is converted into a corresponding $\gamma$ factor. The product of all $\gamma$ factors determines how much the actual growth rate is reduced from the optimal growth rate.

5.2 Source and Meaning of Food Matrix Parameters

The food matrix parameter table provides practical environmental conditions for different ready-to-eat (RTE) food products. These parameters include product type, pH, water activity ($a_w$), storage temperature, preservatives, and packaging conditions. These values are not the final prediction results of the model. Instead, they are used as input variables for the secondary Gamma model.

The general role of the parameter table is to connect real food conditions with mathematical growth prediction. For each food product, the measured or literature-reported values of temperature, pH, $a_w$, nitrite, and organic acid preservatives are converted into corresponding inhibition factors. These inhibition factors are then multiplied together to calculate the predicted maximum specific growth rate of Listeria monocytogenes.

5.4 Example: Cooked Ham

Cooked ham is a typical RTE meat product. According to the parameter table, cooked ham has approximately the following food matrix characteristics: pH = 5.8–6.5, $a_w$ = 0.96–0.99, $T$ = 4°C or 10°C, and may contain lactate, acetate, and nitrite as preservatives.

To use these data in the model, a representative condition can be selected. For example:

$$T = 4^\circ\text{C},\quad \text{pH} = 6.2,\quad a_w = 0.98$$

Assume that the product contains lactate, acetate, and nitrite. These values are then converted into Gamma factors:

$$T = 4^\circ\text{C} \rightarrow \gamma(T)$$

$$\text{pH} = 6.2 \rightarrow \gamma(\text{pH})$$

$$a_w = 0.98 \rightarrow \gamma(a_w)$$

$$\text{Nitrite} \rightarrow \gamma(\text{Nit})$$

$$\text{Lactate + Acetate} \rightarrow \gamma(\text{HA})$$

The final predicted growth rate is:

$$\mu_{\max,\text{ham}} = \mu_{\text{opt}} \cdot \gamma(4) \cdot \gamma(6.2) \cdot \gamma(0.98) \cdot \gamma(\text{Nit}) \cdot \gamma(\text{Lactate}) \cdot \gamma(\text{Acetate}) \cdot \xi$$

This predicted $\mu_{\max,\text{ham}}$ is then inserted into the primary growth model to simulate the growth curve of L. monocytogenes in cooked ham during refrigerated storage.

6. Parameter Estimation and Model Fitting

6.1 Experimental Data

To fit the model, bacterial counts are measured at different time points during storage. The experimental data usually consist of pairs of time and bacterial concentration:

$$(t_1, N_1),\ (t_2, N_2),\ \ldots,\ (t_n, N_n)$$

where $N_i$ is the measured bacterial concentration at time $t_i$. The bacterial concentration may be expressed as $\log_{10}(\text{CFU/g})$ or $\ln(\text{CFU/g})$, depending on the form of the model.

6.2 Primary Model Fitting

The primary model is fitted to the experimental growth curve to estimate:

$$\mu_{\max},\quad t_{\text{lag}},\quad N_0,\quad N_{\max}$$

The fitting is usually performed by nonlinear regression. The objective is to minimize the sum of squared residuals between observed and predicted bacterial counts:

$$\text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_{i,\text{obs}} - y_{i,\text{pred}}\right)^2$$

where:

  • $y_{i,\text{obs}}$ is the observed bacterial concentration;
  • $y_{i,\text{pred}}$ is the predicted bacterial concentration.

6.3 Secondary Model Fitting

After $\mu_{\max}$ values are obtained from multiple growth curves under different environmental conditions, these values are used to fit the secondary model. For each condition, the secondary model predicts:

$$\mu_{\max,\text{pred}} = \mu_{\text{opt}} \cdot \gamma(T) \cdot \gamma(\text{pH}) \cdot \gamma(a_w) \cdot \gamma(\text{Nit}) \cdot \gamma(\text{HA}) \cdot \xi$$

The predicted values are then compared with the experimentally estimated $\mu_{\max}$ values. Model parameters such as $\mu_{\text{opt}}$, $T_{\min}$, $T_{\text{opt}}$, $\text{pH}_{\min}$, $\text{pH}_{\text{opt}}$, $\text{pH}_{\max}$, $a_{w,\min}$, and MIC values can be estimated or adapted from published studies.

7. Model Evaluation

The performance of the model can be evaluated using statistical indicators such as the root mean square error, bias factor, and accuracy factor.

7.1 Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)

$$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_{i,\text{obs}} - y_{i,\text{pred}}\right)^2}$$

A smaller RMSE indicates better agreement between the observed and predicted values.

7.2 Bias Factor ($B_f$)

The bias factor evaluates whether the model systematically overpredicts or underpredicts bacterial growth:

$$B_f = 10^{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log_{10}\left(\frac{y_{i,\text{pred}}}{y_{i,\text{obs}}}\right)}$$

If $B_f$ is close to 1, the model has little systematic bias. If $B_f > 1$, the model tends to overpredict growth. If $B_f < 1$, the model tends to underpredict growth.

7.3 Accuracy Factor ($A_f$)

The accuracy factor describes the average deviation between predicted and observed values:

$$A_f = 10^{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left|\log_{10}\left(\frac{y_{i,\text{pred}}}{y_{i,\text{obs}}}\right)\right|}$$

An accuracy factor closer to 1 indicates better prediction accuracy.

8. Interpretation of Model Outputs

The main output of the complete model is a predicted growth curve of L. monocytogenes under a specific food condition. This curve shows how bacterial concentration changes during storage.

The model can be used to answer questions such as:

  • How fast will L. monocytogenes grow in a specific RTE food?
  • How long is the lag phase under refrigerated storage?
  • How does temperature abuse affect bacterial growth?
  • How strongly do lactate, acetate, diacetate, or nitrite inhibit growth?
  • Which food matrix presents a higher growth risk?
  • How can storage conditions or preservatives be adjusted to reduce risk?

For example, if the predicted $\mu_{\max}$ is high and the lag time is short, the food condition is favorable for L. monocytogenes growth. In contrast, if the predicted $\mu_{\max}$ is low and the lag time is long, the food condition is inhibitory.
