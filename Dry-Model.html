Poreglow LLO Binding Model

Introduction

This summary reorganizes the mechanistic and analytical core of the Poreglow dry-lab analysis: the simplified membrane-binding model; the experimental and modelled roles of cholesterol and pH; interpretation of the theoretical binding curves; local sensitivity analysis; cholesterol–pH interaction; LLO concentration-dependent cholesterol responses; and baseline normalization of fluorescence data.

The revised order follows the model's causal logic. It first defines the apparent binding variable, then explains why cholesterol and pH enter the model, and only afterward interprets the resulting curves and sensitivity outputs. This prevents provisional parameter mappings from being mistaken for direct experimental measurements.

The framework distinguishes among:

  • findings supported directly by published experiments;
  • assumptions and operational definitions introduced for model construction;
  • exploratory outputs generated by the current implementation.

The model is intended to organize mechanistic hypotheses, describe qualitative trends, and guide experimental design. It should not yet be presented as an externally validated quantitative predictor. In particular, apparent membrane binding, oligomerization, functional pore formation, GFP passage, and structural membrane rupture remain distinct processes and should not be inferred from one another without appropriate measurements.

1. Simplified Membrane-Binding Model

1.1 State Variables

The binding component uses the following variables:

  • $B(t)$: apparent fraction of available membrane-binding sites occupied by LLO;
  • $[\text{LLO}]$: effective concentration of soluble LLO;
  • $G_{\text{chol}}$: cholesterol-related gain factor;
  • $G_{\text{pH}}$: pH-related gain factor;
  • $k_{\text{on}}$: apparent association rate constant;
  • $k_{\text{off}}$: apparent dissociation rate constant.

Under a single-state occupancy approximation, binding is represented as:

$$\frac{dB}{dt} = k_{\text{on}} \, G_{\text{chol}} \, G_{\text{pH}} \, [\text{LLO}] \, (1 - B) - k_{\text{off}} \, B$$

The association term is proportional to the unoccupied fraction, $1 - B$, whereas the dissociation term is proportional to the occupied fraction, $B$.

1.2 Quasi-Steady-State Approximation

If membrane binding is sufficiently rapid relative to downstream oligomerization and permeabilization, the binding state may be approximated as quasi-steady:

$$\frac{dB}{dt} \approx 0$$

Defining the dissociation constant as:

$$K_d = \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{k_{\text{on}}}$$

gives:

$$B_{\text{eq}} = \frac{G_{\text{chol}} \, G_{\text{pH}} \, [\text{LLO}]}{K_d + G_{\text{chol}} \, G_{\text{pH}} \, [\text{LLO}]}$$

$K_d$ has units of concentration. If an association constant is used instead, it should be defined as $K_a = 1/K_d$, with units of inverse concentration.

1.3 Interpretation and Limitations

The model is a single-state Langmuir-type approximation. It does not explicitly distinguish:

  • initial monomer binding;
  • membrane-associated oligomerization;
  • prepore and inserted states;
  • complete ring pores and incomplete arc-shaped assemblies;
  • GFP-permeable lesions and structural membrane rupture.

The published SPR analysis used a two-step binding model, and its second step may reflect lateral association or oligomerization of membrane-bound LLO. Those fitted parameters nevertheless require cautious interpretation.[1]

Accordingly, $B$ should be described as a simplified apparent membrane-binding fraction, not as the fraction of complete pores, GFP-releasing liposomes, or structurally ruptured membranes.

2. Cholesterol: Experimental Evidence and Model Representation

2.1 Main Observations Supported by the Literature

The 2007 study supports several conclusions relevant to the Poreglow model:[1]

  • LLO binding to pure DOPC membranes is weak;
  • membrane cholesterol substantially enhances LLO binding;
  • at pH 8.5, stable binding rises markedly when cholesterol reaches approximately 35 mol% or more;
  • binding, membrane insertion, and calcein release all show a relatively narrow cholesterol transition range;
  • sufficiently cholesterol-rich membranes can support LLO binding and permeabilizing structures even at higher pH.

These observations support the language of a cholesterol threshold or gate-like response. They do not, however, establish that the particular gain function, interpolation rule, or threshold shape used in the current implementation is uniquely correct.

2.2 Cholesterol Affects More Than Initial Attachment

The M344 antibody inhibits LLO oligomerization and membrane permeabilization while still allowing LLO to bind liposomes containing 35 mol% cholesterol. In the presence of M344, the monomer-associated dissociation rate was approximately:[1]

$$k_{\text{off,mono}} \approx 10.9 \times 10^{-3} \ \text{s}^{-1}$$

Without M344, the apparent slow dissociation rate was approximately:[1]

$$k_{\text{off,oligo}} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-3} \ \text{s}^{-1}$$

Their ratio is:

$$\frac{k_{\text{off,oligo}}}{k_{\text{off,mono}}} \approx 0.128$$

Thus, membrane-associated oligomerization is linked to an apparent dissociation process that is about 7.8-fold slower. The non-antibody trace was not well represented by a simple single-exponential fit, so the slow phase should not be interpreted as the intrinsic dissociation constant of one homogeneous oligomeric species.[1]

3. pH: Separating Binding Effects from Temperature-Dependent Inactivation

3.1 Approximately 20–25 °C: pH Changes Activity and Event Frequency

At approximately 20–25 °C, where rapid aggregation is limited, LLO did not show a major detectable difference in overall structure between pH 5.5 and 7.5, yet haemolytic and membrane-permeabilizing activities were higher at lower pH. The authors attributed much of this difference to altered membrane binding rather than to the formation of fundamentally different pore types.[2]

Planar lipid-membrane experiments further indicated that:[2]

  • more pore-forming events occurred at pH 5.5 than at pH 7.5;
  • pore conductance had a broad distribution at both pH values;
  • no clear pH-dependent difference in pore size was detected;
  • changing cholesterol from 20% to 35% altered LLO activity without establishing a distinct pore-size shift.

Accordingly, the model should not assume that neutral pH always improves binding while acidic pH acts only on downstream pore formation. Under the 20 mol% cholesterol SPR conditions reported in 2007, lower pH was associated with greater and more stable binding.[1]

3.2 At 37 °C: Neutral or Alkaline pH Promotes Inactivating Aggregation

At pH 7.5 and 37 °C, LLO rapidly forms amorphous aggregates. Aggregated LLO binds liposomes poorly and loses pore-forming activity. At pH 5.5, self-association or dimer-related states may help protect LLO from premature inactivation.[2]

The model should therefore separate at least three processes:

  1. reversible membrane binding over short times or at lower temperature;
  2. membrane-associated oligomerization and pore formation;
  3. irreversible solution-phase aggregation governed jointly by temperature and pH.

A single pH gain factor cannot fully represent all three mechanisms. In the current simplified model, $G_{\text{pH}}$ should be understood as a provisional effective factor whose meaning depends on the experimental temperature and observation window.

5. Local Sensitivity Analysis

5.1 Reference Condition

The current reference condition is: $C_{\text{chol}} = 40\ \text{mol\%}$, pH = 6.5, $[\text{LLO}] = 10\ \text{nM}$.

At this point, the model predicts:

$$B_{\text{eq}} \approx 0.551$$

5.2 Local Sensitivity to Cholesterol

The normalized local sensitivity of $B$ to cholesterol is defined as:

$$S_{B,C} = \frac{C}{B} \cdot \frac{\partial B}{\partial C}$$

Under the current provisional calibration:

$$S_{B,C} \approx 2.43$$

Near the reference point, a 1% relative increase in cholesterol mol% is therefore associated with an approximately 2.43% relative increase in the predicted value of $B$, with all other inputs fixed.

This is a local, model-dependent quantity. It depends on the reference point, the cholesterol-to-$K_d$ mapping, the interpolation procedure, and the assumed model structure. It must not be interpreted as a constant biological effect across the full cholesterol range.

5.3 Local Sensitivity to LLO Concentration

The normalized local sensitivity to LLO concentration is:

$$S_{B,\text{LLO}} = \frac{[\text{LLO}]}{B} \cdot \frac{\partial B}{\partial [\text{LLO}]}$$

At the reference point:

$$S_{B,\text{LLO}} \approx 0.449$$

Thus, a 1% relative increase in LLO concentration produces an approximately 0.449% relative increase in predicted $B$ near this condition. The sensitivity decreases as the binding response approaches saturation.

5.4 Reporting Sensitivity to pH

A percentage sensitivity is inappropriate for pH because pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen-ion activity. The model should instead report either:

$$\frac{\partial B}{\partial \text{pH}}$$

or the predicted $B$ for a clearly stated absolute pH increment, such as 0.1 pH unit. Because $G_{\text{pH}}$ has not yet been robustly fitted, current pH-sensitivity values should be treated as demonstrations of model behavior rather than established biological estimates.

6. Cholesterol–pH Interaction

The interaction plot fixes $[\text{LLO}] = 10\ \text{nM}$ and uses color to represent the predicted apparent binding fraction $B$. The dominant gradient is strongly influenced by the provisional cholesterol-dependent $K_d$ mapping.

Under the current parameterization, the model output changes more strongly across the cholesterol axis than across the provisional pH mapping.

This statement is deliberately model-specific. The heat map alone does not establish that cholesterol is biologically more important than pH under all conditions, that high cholesterol fully compensates for every pH effect, or that pH cannot shift the apparent cholesterol threshold. Published results support interdependence between cholesterol and pH, but any claim of compensation requires a defined response variable and matched comparison conditions.[1]

7. Cholesterol Response at Different LLO Concentrations

The comparison uses representative LLO concentrations of 0.1, 10, and 1000 nM. In the Langmuir-type expression, increasing LLO raises the effective concentration term:

$$G_{\text{chol}} \, G_{\text{pH}} \, [\text{LLO}]$$

Consequently:

  • high LLO concentrations drive $B$ toward saturation across a broader cholesterol range;
  • lower LLO concentrations leave the predicted response more sensitive to the assumed $K_d$ and cholesterol mapping;
  • an apparent shift in the cholesterol response can arise from mass action combined with the imposed parameter mapping, rather than from a newly demonstrated molecular threshold.

The concentrations 0.1, 10, and 1000 nM are illustrative values selected to compare model behavior. Without direct evidence from the target biological setting, they should not be labelled as subtoxic, physiologically infectious, or saturating concentrations.

8. Fluorescence Calibration Using Baseline Normalization

8.1 Selected Normalization Method

The revised workflow uses ROI-wise baseline normalization instead of making an experimentally measured $F_{\max}$ the default denominator. The method is intended for single-liposome or ROI time series and does not require a separate maximum-release measurement for every trace.[3,4]

First, subtract non-target background:

$$F_{\text{corr},i}(t) = F_{\text{raw},i}(t) - B_i(t)$$

$B_i(t)$ may be estimated from a matched liposome-free region, a dark/reference acquisition, or a validated local-background model.

Next, estimate the baseline over a predefined pre-treatment interval $T_0$:

$$F_{0,i} = \underset{t \in T_0}{\text{median}}\ F_{\text{corr},i}(t)$$

Finally, calculate:

$$R_i(t) = \frac{F_{\text{corr},i}(t) - F_{0,i}}{F_{0,i}}$$

The median is preferred when the baseline may contain occasional transients or outliers. A mean may be used if the baseline is stable and free of such events. The same background and baseline-window rules should be applied across the dataset, while each liposome or ROI retains its own $F_{0,i}$.[3,4]

8.2 Defining a Positive Release-Associated Response

If the analysis tracks intraliposomal GFP, cargo loss normally appears as decreasing fluorescence. To make greater putative release correspond to a positive value, define normalized intraliposomal fluorescence loss as:

$$D_i(t) = \frac{F_{0,i} - F_{\text{corr},i}(t)}{F_{0,i}} = -R_i(t)$$

Interpretation:

  • $D_i = 0$: no change from the corrected pre-treatment baseline;
  • $D_i > 0$: intraliposomal fluorescence has decreased relative to baseline;
  • $D_i = 0.8$: corrected intraliposomal fluorescence is 80% below its initial baseline.

If the experiment instead measures increasing fluorescence in the external medium, the positive $R(t) = \Delta F / F_0$ convention may be used directly. The method must state the ROI location, signal direction, and background definition.

8.3 Advantages Relative to $F_{\max}$ Normalization

Baseline normalization offers several practical advantages:

  • it avoids a separate detergent or maximum-response calibration for every trace;
  • it avoids relying on an $F_{\max}$ that may be distorted by detector saturation, detergent-dependent optical effects, endpoint drift, or failure to reach a true maximum;
  • it scales each trajectory to its own initial brightness, reducing differences associated with loading, illumination, focus, and collection efficiency;
  • it is well suited to time-resolved microscopy, repeated measurements, and large numbers of ROIs;
  • a baseline median is less sensitive to noise than a single initial frame.[3,4]

8.4 Limitations and Trade-offs

The method also imposes important limitations:

  • $\Delta F / F_0$ is a relative change from baseline, not an absolute fraction of total releasable GFP;
  • the same value of $D$ need not correspond to the same physical endpoint across samples;
  • background-subtraction errors can produce $D > 1$ or anomalous negative values;
  • a small $F_0$ amplifies modest background errors;
  • photobleaching, focus drift, and liposome movement can mimic intraliposomal fluorescence loss;
  • if no stable pre-treatment interval exists, estimating $F_0$ requires a percentile, rolling baseline, or model-based procedure that adds assumptions;
  • without $F_{\max}$, $D$ alone cannot establish complete release or make measurements from different indicators and instruments directly equivalent.[3,4]

Accordingly, baseline normalization exchanges an absolute maximum-referenced scale for a simpler ROI-specific relative-response measure. If the goal is to estimate the fraction of all releasable cargo, detergent-defined $F_{\max}$ remains informative and may be retained for representative validation samples rather than used as the default denominator for every trajectory.

8.5 Minimum Quality-Control Requirements

A defensible analysis should specify in advance:

  • the pre-treatment baseline interval $T_0$;
  • how background ROIs are selected and whether background is time varying;
  • exclusion rules for saturated traces, near-zero $F_0$, unstable baselines, excessive drift, and poor signal-to-noise ratio;
  • photobleaching correction, supported by no-LLO controls;
  • liposome tracking and focal-drift correction;
  • any threshold used to define near-complete fluorescence loss and the minimum duration for which it must persist.

A rolling baseline may help correct slow drift, but its window must be substantially longer than the biological response of interest. Otherwise, genuine slow release may be absorbed into the estimated baseline.

Conclusion

The selected Poreglow framework provides a coherent but deliberately simplified connection between cholesterol, pH, temperature-dependent LLO availability, apparent membrane binding, and fluorescence-based readouts. Published evidence supports strong cholesterol dependence and a context-dependent pH effect, but neither variable acts through a single isolated step. Cholesterol influences binding and membrane-associated assembly, whereas pH affects binding as well as temperature-dependent solution-phase aggregation and inactivation.

The binding variable $B$ represents apparent occupancy under a Langmuir-type approximation; it is not a direct measure of oligomer number, inserted pores, GFP release, or membrane rupture. Consequently, the theoretical cholesterol and pH responses remain conditional on provisional parameter mappings. The reported sensitivities are local properties of the present model rather than universal biological constants, and the single factor $G_{\text{pH}}$ cannot yet distinguish reversible binding effects from irreversible thermal inactivation.

The fluorescence workflow should likewise be interpreted operationally. ROI-wise baseline normalization provides a practical way to compare time-dependent responses without measuring $F_{\max}$ for every trace. For intraliposomal GFP, the derived variable $D_i(t)$ quantifies fluorescence loss relative to the corrected pre-treatment baseline. It does not, by itself, quantify the absolute fraction of cargo released or prove structural membrane rupture.

The most defensible use of the model is therefore to generate testable trends and organize experimental comparisons while preserving strict separation among published evidence, modelling assumptions, and exploratory predictions. Future validation should refit the cholesterol and pH mappings using original data, retain raw and normalized fluorescence traces, include controls for bleaching and motion, use representative $F_{\max}$ measurements where absolute release matters, and compare predicted responses against experimental conditions not used for calibration.

Recommended Cholesterol Concentrations

Executive Summary

This report converts the cholesterol-dependent observations in three core LLO studies into practical concentration ranges for the Poreglow model and related wet-lab experiments. The central conclusion is that cholesterol should not be treated as a simple linear multiplier. Instead, the literature supports a progression from negligible productive interaction at very low cholesterol, through a condition-dependent intermediate region, into a relatively narrow transition around 30–35 mol%, followed by a high-response regime at approximately 35–50 mol%.[1–3]

For communication and experimental planning, the recommended interpretation is:

Table 2: Recommended interpretation of cholesterol ranges.
Cholesterol concentrationPredicted Bind StateRecommended use

10–20 mol%LowWeak-binding or edge-interaction condition
20–30 mol%Low to moderateCondition-dependent binding and early activity
30–35 mol%TransitionBest range for resolving the cholesterol-dependent switch

40–50 mol%Very highStrong-response or positive-control condition

The term Bind state is used here as an operational, normalized description of productive membrane association. It is not claimed to be a direct molecular occupancy or the fraction of complete pores.

1. Scope and Interpretation

LLO action involves several separable stages: soluble toxin availability, initial membrane attachment, retention at the membrane, lateral oligomerization, prepore formation, membrane insertion, pore formation, and larger-scale membrane damage. Consequently, SPR response, monolayer insertion pressure, calcein release, pore-event frequency, and membrane-area destruction are not interchangeable measurements.[1–3]

The present analysis therefore uses the studies in a layered way:

  1. Bavdek et al. 2007 provides the primary cholesterol concentration series and the closest experimental evidence for a cholesterol-dependent Bind state.[1]
  2. Bavdek et al. 2012 clarifies how pH and temperature alter active LLO availability and pore-event frequency, preventing cholesterol effects from being interpreted without environmental context.[2]
  3. Ruan et al. 2016 visualizes oligomerization, insertion, arc formation, and membrane destruction, providing downstream mechanistic validation for the Bind-state interpretation.[3]

No Hill equation is imposed. Shape-preserving interpolation is used only to connect literature-informed points and display the observed trends without introducing a specific mechanistic curve shape.

Bavdek 2012 provides environmental and mechanistic constraints. Ruan 2016 provides downstream validation through direct visualization of oligomerization, insertion, and membrane destruction.

3. Construction and Interpretation of the Three Key Charts

3.1 Cholesterol–Bind Relationship

How the chart was created:

  1. The usable Bavdek 2007 cholesterol conditions were organized separately for stable SPR binding, monolayer insertion, and calcein release.
  2. Each endpoint was normalized independently to a 0–1 scale. This prevents incomparable physical units, such as resonance units, pressure, and percentage release, from being combined directly.
  3. A shape-preserving PCHIP interpolator was applied to each endpoint series. PCHIP was selected because it connects the literature-informed points smoothly while limiting artificial overshoot and avoiding the mechanistic assumptions of a Hill fit.
  4. At every cholesterol concentration, the median of the available endpoint curves was calculated as the operational Bind-state trend.
  5. The lowest and highest endpoint values were retained as a shaded envelope. This envelope shows that initial association, insertion, and release do not respond identically to cholesterol.

What the chart represents: The solid curve represents the consensus progression from weak or nonproductive membrane interaction to stable and productive membrane association. The curve is shallow below approximately 20 mol%, begins to rise between approximately 20 and 30 mol%, changes most strongly around 30–35 mol%, and enters a high-response region above approximately 35–40 mol%.

The chart must not be interpreted as an experimentally measured fractional occupancy. The operational $B$ value summarizes agreement among related assays and is most useful for comparing concentration ranges.

3.2 Comparison with Downstream Membrane Disruption

How the chart was created:

  1. The operational Bind-state curve from the previous chart was retained without refitting.
  2. Ruan's approximate membrane-destruction velocities at 0, 10, 20, and 40 mol% cholesterol were divided by the maximum reported value, producing a normalized 0–1 downstream-response series.
  3. PCHIP interpolation connected the Ruan points only within the measured 0–40 mol% range.
  4. The normalized Ruan curve was plotted beside the Bavdek-derived operational Bind state. The two curves were not pooled into a single fit because they describe different physical outputs.

What the chart represents: The comparison emphasizes that membrane destruction and stable bulk membrane association are related but not identical. At 10 mol% cholesterol, Ruan observed edge-mediated damage even though typical arc formation was absent. At 20 mol%, oligomerization and insertion became observable. At 40 mol%, both productive association and downstream destruction were strong.[3]

The result supports a staged interpretation: initial association → retention and oligomerization → insertion → pore or edge-mediated damage. It also explains why a low-to-moderate Bind state can still produce a measurable wet-lab response when the assay is sensitive to membrane defects.

3.3 Recommended Cholesterol Ranges

What the chart represents:

  • 0–10 mol%: very low productive Bind state. Cholesterol-free membranes provide a negative control, although 10 mol% may permit some edge-associated damage under favorable acidic conditions.
  • 10–20 mol%: low Bind state. Initial or transient membrane interactions may occur, but typical oligomerization and stable insertion are restricted.
  • 20–30 mol%: low-to-moderate Bind state. Association and activity become detectable but remain strongly dependent on pH, temperature, LLO concentration, membrane geometry, and assay endpoint.
  • 30–35 mol%: transition region. Stable association and downstream competence increase rapidly. This is the most informative range for studying cholesterol dependence.
  • 35–40 mol%: high productive Bind state. Stable association, oligomerization, membrane insertion, and functional permeabilization are increasingly likely.
  • 40–50 mol%: very high response. This range is suitable for strong positive controls and robust pore-forming or membrane-damaging conditions, although benefits beyond 40 mol% may depend on the endpoint.

4. Recommended Cholesterol Concentrations

4.1 Recommended Range for Routine Productive Binding

For experiments requiring robust LLO membrane association and downstream activity, the recommended cholesterol concentration is 35–40 mol%. This range lies beyond the main transition observed in Bavdek 2007 and is consistent with the strong oligomerization and membrane destruction observed by Ruan at 40 mol%.[1,3]

4.2 Recommended Range for Resolving the Cholesterol Transition

For experiments intended to characterize cholesterol concentration dependence, the recommended range is 20–40 mol%. Recommended individual cholesterol levels are 20, 25, 30, 35, and 40 mol%. This design samples the low-to-moderate Bind state, the main transition region, and the high-response state without requiring unsupported extrapolation.

4.4 Conditions That Must Be Reported Alongside Cholesterol

Every cholesterol recommendation should be accompanied by:

  • lipid composition and vesicle or membrane geometry;
  • pH during LLO preincubation and during measurement;
  • temperature and preincubation duration;
  • total and, where possible, active LLO concentration;
  • assay endpoint, such as SPR binding, insertion, cargo release, pore events, or structural membrane loss;
  • observation time and normalization procedure.

Without these variables, the same cholesterol concentration may correspond to different apparent Bind states.

5. Review of the Initial Binding Model

The initial Poreglow binding model represents a single apparent membrane-bound state:

$$\frac{dB}{dt} = k_{\text{on}} \, G_{\text{chol}} \, G_{\text{pH}} \, [\text{LLO}] \, (1 - B) - k_{\text{off}} \, B$$

Under a rapid-equilibrium approximation:

$$B_{\text{eq}} = \frac{G_{\text{chol}} \, G_{\text{pH}} \, [\text{LLO}]}{K_D + G_{\text{chol}} \, G_{\text{pH}} \, [\text{LLO}]}, \qquad K_D = \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{k_{\text{on}}}$$

This formulation is useful because it is compact, interpretable, and suitable for exploratory dry-lab simulations. However, the literature review shows that a single state cannot separately represent initial attachment, retention, oligomerization, prepore formation, insertion, functional pores, and membrane destruction.[1–3] The present cholesterol analysis therefore contributes a more cautious interpretation of $G_{\text{chol}}$ and $B$:

  • $G_{\text{chol}}$ should be viewed as an empirical mapping from physical cholesterol mol% to productive membrane permissiveness.
  • $B$ should be described as a simplified apparent Bind state, not as the fraction of complete pores or ruptured membranes.
  • A slower apparent $k_{\text{off}}$ at higher cholesterol provides the kinetic link between cholesterol concentration and greater stable association.
  • Ruan's observations support adding explicit downstream states in future versions of the model.

A natural future extension is:

$$\text{LLO}_{\text{soluble}} \rightarrow \text{LLO}_{\text{bound}} \rightarrow \text{Oligomer/Prepore} \rightarrow \text{Inserted pore} \rightarrow \text{Membrane endpoint}$$

with an additional solution-phase inactivation route governed by pH, temperature, and time.[2]

6. Final Recommendations

  1. Use 35–40 mol% cholesterol for robust productive LLO binding and membrane activity.
  2. Use 20–40 mol% to characterize the cholesterol dependence, with denser sampling at 25, 30, and 35 mol%.
  3. Use 0 mol% as a negative control and 40 mol% as the principal positive control.
  4. Treat 10–20 mol% as a low-Bind but not necessarily zero-activity condition, because edge-mediated damage may occur under favorable conditions.
  5. Interpret 30–35 mol% as the key transition range, not as a universal molecular threshold.
  6. Report pH, temperature, LLO concentration, membrane composition, and endpoint together with cholesterol concentration.
  7. Retain the initial binding model as a simplified dry-lab layer, but avoid equating $B$ with pore number, GFP release, or membrane rupture.
